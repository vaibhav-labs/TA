[H1] TA Arena — Game-Based Learning Strategy (PWA)
·
v2, 12 Oct 2026. The four PDFs are all Transactional Analysis (TA) texts, so this is a TA mastery game — but v1 specified it as a catalogue of levels, which is why the built screen shows a card of text and "Coming soon". v2 fixes that at the root: the game is now a seven-week story with a fixed cast, every level is a fully scripted scene with meters and consequences, every book term has a plain in-game word (the real term is revealed only after the player has felt it), and the reward for learning is literally seeing more of each conversation. Sections 2a–2c and 6 are new; 4 and 10 are amended; the rest stands.

How the four books map onto one curriculum. TA Today supplies the order (it is a textbook). Berne 1961 supplies the deep theory for Worlds 1–2. Games People Play supplies the scenarios for Worlds 3–4 and the autonomy endgame. Steiner supplies Worlds 5–6 (strokes as economy, scripts as decisions, the Rescue triangle) and the social-critique layer that keeps advanced players interested.
[H2] 2. Design philosophy
The game teaches by making the player do TA, not read it: every level drops the player into a live transaction, asks for a diagnosis or a move, shows the consequence, then names the concept. Theory arrives after the experience, never before. This mirrors how Berne taught — observe the behaviour, then label the ego state.
Three player levels, one path. Every world is playable at three difficulties that change the question, not the scenario:
Level
Who it is for
What a level asks
Scoring
Spot (L1)
Never heard of TA
Recognise: "Which ego state spoke?" "Was that a stroke?" 2–3 options, immediate feedback.
Right / wrong, 1 point
Play (L2)
Knows the basics, wants fluency
Intervene: "Pick the next line that keeps communication open" / "cross this transaction on purpose". 3–4 options, branching consequence.
Best / okay / game-move, 0–3 points
Analyse (L3)
Coach, counsellor, TA student
Explain: fill the formal analysis (aim, roles, paradigm, payoff, antithesis), name the injunction behind the script, design the contract. Free-text or multi-field, graded by rubric.
Rubric 0–5 per field
A player picks a level at the start; the app recommends moving up when accuracy on a world passes 85 %, and down (quietly) when it falls under 50 %.
The learning loop every level follows (the engine, not the content):
Situation — a 3–8 line dialogue or inner monologue, named characters, stated setting.
Move — the player diagnoses, chooses a line, or fills an analysis.
Consequence — the scene plays one more beat showing what that move produced.
Lesson card — the concept, the book it comes from, one quotable definition, the common confusion.
Echo — 24–72 h later the same concept returns in a new situation (spaced retrieval, section 8).
Why games, and why these games. Berne's own word for the pathological patterns is "games", so the app leans into the pun: the player's job is to stop playing Berne's games by getting good at this one. Each of Berne's named games becomes a scenario the player must first recognise, then refuse (the antithesis), then explain. That gives the thesaurus of ~36 games a natural three-pass structure that maps exactly onto Spot / Play / Analyse.
Design constraints agreed with you:
Visuals stay minimal: text, one colour accent, simple PAC stacked-circle diagrams drawn in CSS, no illustrations, no animation beyond a 150 ms fade.
Depth over breadth per level: a single level teaches one concept and one confusion.
Roundedness: every concept is met at least three times — once as recognition, once as a decision, once as explanation — and across at least two books' framings where they differ (e.g. Berne's Rescuer vs Steiner's Rescue triangle vs Karpman's Drama Triangle).
[H2] 2a. The story
You are 27 and you have just moved to Pune for a job. Seven weeks of your life play out, one week per world, across three places: the flat you share, the office you've joined, and the phone calls home. Everyone you meet is running a pattern they can't see. By week seven you can see all of them — and your own.
Setting. Kesari Heights, a four-storey building in Kothrud. You rent a room in flat 3B. Downstairs in 1A lives the landlady. Your job is at Lumen, a 40-person software company in Baner, a 35-minute ride away. Home is a city 600 km away, reachable only by phone. Every scene is stamped with a day, a time and a place, and the week runs Monday to Sunday.
The frame. Each Sunday you go to a drop-in group at a café called Chai & Why, run by Meera. Meera is the game's teacher. She never lectures before a scene; she debriefs after it, in plain words, and she gives you one thing to watch for in the coming week. The Sunday session is the week's boss: a live scene where you have to use what the week taught, with Meera watching.

[H2] 2b. Plain words
The player never meets a book term before they have lived the thing it names. Every concept has an in-game word that an 8-year-old would understand; the book term appears only on Meera's debrief card, after the scene, as "the books call this…". From then on the app uses both, plain word first. This is the single rule that makes it a learning game instead of a textbook with buttons.

[H2] 2c. What makes it a game
A screen of text with a "next" button is a book. Five mechanics turn the same text into play: something to lose, something to see, something that remembers, something to build, and a weekly test under pressure.
1. Two meters on every scene. Top of the screen, two thin bars.
Them — how the other person is with you right now. Starts mid; a line that lands warm raises it, a cold one drops it, a trap-move spikes it (traps feel great for a beat) then crashes it at the sting.
You — your own steadiness. Drops when you answer from a voice you were trained to rather than chose; drops hard when you take the Saviour chair; recovers on a cut that works, on a nod you accept, on a real line.
Meters move visibly on every choice, with a one-word tag ("warm", "cold", "hook", "cut", "real"). A scene ends early if Them hits zero (they walk off, slam a door, go quiet) or You hits zero (you cave, say yes to something you'll resent). Either early end is a valid outcome and the debrief uses it. The meters are the player's first instrument: before they can name anything, they can feel a line go wrong.
2. The Reveal layer. This is the game's reward curve. The conversation is shown plain at first; as the player learns, they unlock the ability to see what is under it, by long-pressing any line.

Nothing is shown before it is unlocked, so a week-1 player sees a clean chat and a week-6 player sees every line annotated. Replaying week 1 with week-6 eyes is a stated goal of the game ("Go back and watch Rohan's first apology now").
3. Carried consequences. Each scene writes flags; later scenes read them. Examples: if in 1.3 you forgave Rohan's spill with "it's fine", then in 4.1 he opens with a bigger spill and the line "you're the only one who gets me"; if you refused it, 4.1 opens with him sulking at Tanvi instead and you watch the trap from the outside. If you let Aai's yes-but run to the silence in 1.5, the week-4 boss has a sixth voice (Aai) at the table. The flags are few (≤ 3 per scene) and named in each script.
4. Things you build and keep. Your voice-print (from week 2, updated every scene); your nod ledger (week 1); your Trap Book — a page per trap you've named, with where you saw it (week 4 on); your Plan Board (week 6, built from your own play history, not a fictional one); your Deal with Tanvi (week 7). All visible on the profile, all exportable.
5. Sunday under pressure. The boss scene has a visible timer per line (12 s at Play level, none at Spot, 20 s at Analyse because you must also write), the meters are twice as sensitive, and Meera interrupts once mid-scene to ask "What just happened?" — a one-tap diagnosis the player must get right to continue. Pass = Them above 50 % at the end and You above 50 %. You can fail; the week doesn't advance; Meera gives one hint and you replay.
What this changes in the engines (section 5). Every engine takes meters and flags in and emits deltas; Choice-story becomes the default engine for ~80 % of scenes with the others (Sort-it, Counter, Ladder, Build, Vector) appearing as moments inside a scene — a 20-second mini-board that the scene pauses for — rather than as whole levels. Section 10's schema is amended accordingly.
