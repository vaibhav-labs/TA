You

Forty-two short games about how people talk to each other, and why you talk the way you do.

You is a set of games built on Transactional Analysis, or TA, a way of understanding conversations that the psychiatrist Eric Berne began in the 1950s. Each game puts you inside a small story: a phone call with your mum, a meeting that goes sideways, an argument over the washing-up. You make a choice, then find out what it really did.

  • SpotNotice what is going on in a short exchange.
  • PlayStep into a live scene and choose what to say.
  • AnalyseWork out why it went the way it did.

Every answer, right or wrong, comes with a plain explanation and a real example. A game takes about ten minutes, and you need no background in psychology.

The games follow books by Eric Berne, Claude Steiner, and Ian Stewart and Vann Joines. They teach ideas. They are not therapy.

Being Noticed

Every person needs small signs that they matter: a hello, a smile, a nod, even a cross look. Psychologists call these signs strokes. This world is about spotting them. Which ones do you give, which ones do you take, and what does a day with almost none feel like?

Why it comes hereThis world comes first because the rest of the game is built on small moments between people. Strokes are the first thing to notice.
After this world you cantell a warm nod from a cold one, and notice what a day with almost none costs you.

Final challenge: Break the Nod Rules: break the five unwritten rules people use to hand out nods, and say who benefits from them

Who’s Talking

Inside each of us there are three voices. The Boss repeats the rules we were taught as children. The Pilot works out what is true today. The Kid feels and wants, the way it did when we were small. This world trains your ear to hear which one is speaking.

Why it comes hereThis world comes second because once you can see the small moments between people, the next question is who is talking. The same words can come from any of the three voices.
After this world you canname which voice is talking, from the words, the tone and the posture.

Back and Forth

Every conversation is a string of throws and catches. One person says something aimed at one of your voices, and you answer from one of yours. Some replies keep the talk going. Others cut it off. This world shows you where each line is aimed and where the answer comes from.

Why it comes hereThis world comes third because you need to know the three voices first. Only then can you tell where a line starts and where it lands.
After this world you candraw the arrow for any line, and say whether the reply matches it or cuts across it.

Final challenge: The Friendly Sales Line: find the sting in a friendly sales line, and answer both of its targets

World 4 of 7

Conversation Traps

Some arguments and awkward chats keep coming back with new faces and the same ending. Psychologists call them games. Here we call them traps, because each one has a hook, a switch and a payoff that nobody wanted. You learn to spot the trap and step out of it.

Why it comes hereThis world comes fourth because once you can follow a line and its reply, you can notice when the same exchange keeps coming back. A repeating exchange is a trap, and this world gives each one a name.
After this world you cantell a trap from real talk by listening for the hook, the switch and what the trap pays.

Final challenge: The Yes-But Loop: step out of a loop of yes-buts without leaving the room

Under the Surface

Under many traps is a feeling that does not fit the moment: a sting, a grudge, a guilt that never goes away. Some people save these feelings like loyalty stamps, to cash in later. This world teaches you to tell a real feeling from a stand-in one.

Why it comes hereThis world comes fifth because you can only find the real feeling once you have spotted the trap that is covering it.
After this world you cantell a real feeling, which finishes something, from a stand-in feeling, which never does.

Final challenge: Kind but Stuck: see the kind-sounding line that keeps a trap going, and say what a real step would look like

The Story You Wrote

Many people live by a plan they made as children. It says what they must do, what they must never do, and how their story should end. When the same traps keep coming back, that plan is usually behind them. This world teaches you to read it.

Why it comes hereThis world comes sixth because the traps add up to a plan. You need to know the traps before you can see the plan they build.
After this world you canhear the rule running under a line, and read the plan a life story is following.

Final challenge: The Plan Board: place a life story’s rules and plan on one board, and suggest a new line that can be acted on this month

Free to Choose

Being free means choosing your own moves instead of replaying the ones you learned as a child. In this world you learn to help without rescuing, make clear agreements, say yes with a safety net, and pick the reply that is truly yours.

Why it comes hereThis world comes last because once you can see your plan, you can change it. The worlds before this one help you see. This one helps you act.
After this world you canhelp without rescuing, make clear agreements, and keep a hard conversation honest.
